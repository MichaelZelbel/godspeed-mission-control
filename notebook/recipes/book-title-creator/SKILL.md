# Book Title Creator

## Purpose

Turn a book's market promise into titles and subtitles that the right reader can understand, want, remember, repeat, and trust.

A title is not decoration. It is a compact positioning decision. Optimize for the reader's response, not the author's attachment to a clever phrase.

Strong commercial nonfiction titles usually trigger at least one useful reaction:

- "I want that result."
- "That is exactly my problem."
- "This was written for someone like me."
- "I need to understand that idea."

Never claim that a title guarantees commercial success. Treat every candidate as a marketing hypothesis that still needs conflict checks and real-reader validation.

## Scope

This skill is optimized for commercial nonfiction, including business, self-help, how-to, professional, technical, practical, memoir-with-a-promise, and thought-leadership books.

For fiction, poetry, or highly literary work, adapt the method toward genre signal, emotional promise, atmosphere, intrigue, and memorability. State that the nonfiction scoring weights do not transfer perfectly.

## Choose the working mode

Infer the mode from the request rather than making the user choose.

### Full strategy

Use when the user wants a serious naming process or a publishable shortlist. Build the positioning brief, inspect the current competitive landscape when tools are available, generate broadly, score, red-team, and propose validation.

### Fast sprint

Use when the user wants ideas quickly and has supplied enough context. State only the assumptions that materially affect the answer, then produce strategically diverse candidates. Do not bury the user in process.

### Audit and compare

Use when the user supplies one or more existing titles. Diagnose each candidate before rewriting it. Preserve what works. Explain trade-offs rather than replacing everything merely to appear creative.

### Subtitle rescue

Use when the main title is fixed. Create subtitle directions that add missing audience, outcome, mechanism, scope, or credibility without repeating the title.

### Validation

Use when the user wants to test titles or interpret reader-test results. Measure comprehension, audience recognition, credibility, recall, and behavior, not preference alone.

## Interaction rules

- Reuse all relevant information already supplied.
- Ask only for information that could materially change the naming strategy.
- When clarification is essential, ask the single highest-leverage question and stop.
- When the user requests immediate output, state reasonable assumptions and proceed.
- When the user requests step-by-step work, provide only the current stage and stop.
- Do not fabricate market research, title availability, trademarks, sales data, or legal clearance.
- When browsing is available and the decision matters, research current comparable titles, reader language, exact-title conflicts, and marketplace rules.
- Distinguish verified findings from inference.
- Treat model scores as structured judgment, not proof of demand.
- Avoid em dashes unless the user explicitly requests them.

## Core principles

### 1. Start with the reader's desired movement

A nonfiction book is usually purchased because the reader wants movement:

- toward a result
- away from pain, cost, risk, or confusion
- into a valued identity
- toward mastery, agency, or control
- toward a new explanation of something important

A useful diagnostic question is:

> Does this title express something the reader wants, fears, recognizes, or urgently wants to understand?

A title that merely labels the subject usually forces the subtitle and description to do too much work.

### 2. Do not confuse the subject with the promise

"A book about AI context management" is a subject.

"Build an AI that remembers how you work" is a promise.

The **destination** is what the reader wants. The **vehicle** is the author's framework, technology, method, or process. Lead with the destination or tension unless the vehicle is already desirable, genuinely distinctive, or inherently intriguing.

### 3. Use the reader's language

Prefer words the intended reader already uses for the pain, desired result, failed alternatives, and emotional meaning of success. Avoid author-centric jargon unless it is a useful category signal for the target audience.

When research is possible, mine patterns from reviews, communities, interviews, search suggestions, sales calls, and support conversations. Do not copy a competitor's distinctive wording.

### 4. Give title and subtitle different jobs

The title usually earns **attention and recall**. It may lead with an outcome, problem, identity, big idea, mechanism, or curiosity.

The subtitle usually earns **comprehension and trust**. It can add audience, outcome, mechanism, scope, proof, or a credible boundary.

A subtitle that merely restates the title wastes valuable cover space.

### 5. Prefer memorable clarity

Clarity over cleverness is a guardrail, not a command to be dull. Add memory through rhythm, alliteration, contrast, balanced structure, concrete imagery, or a compact unexpected phrase.

Read every serious candidate aloud. A title must work when spoken, recommended, searched, and remembered.

### 6. Make specificity earn its keep

Numbers, timeframes, absolutes, superlatives, and "without" promises increase attention and proof burden. Never invent precision merely because it strengthens the headline.

Ask:

- Can the manuscript support the claim?
- Is the qualifier visible enough?
- Does the specificity simplify the promise or clutter it?
- Would a reasonable reader feel misled?

### 7. Balance category signal and distinction

The reader should recognize roughly what shelf the book belongs on, but the title also needs a reason to be remembered among neighboring books.

Maximum novelty can become incomprehensible. Maximum familiarity can become invisible. Aim for recognizable relevance plus a defensible difference.

### 8. Generate before judging

Separate divergent ideation from evaluation. Premature scoring makes an LLM converge on safe variations of its first idea.

### 9. Preserve strategic diversity

Five synonyms are not five strategies. A useful shortlist contains different ways to win, such as direct benefit, pain relief, identity, authority, curiosity, a distinctive mechanism, or category ownership.

### 10. Treat the title as a hypothesis

The working hypothesis is:

> This wording will attract the intended reader, create the intended expectation, remain credible, and be remembered better than the alternatives.

Reasoning and scoring narrow uncertainty. Reader or behavioral testing provides evidence.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Build the minimum viable naming brief

Establish the following explicitly or by reasonable inference:

1. **Primary reader:** Who is most likely to buy, recommend, or act on the book?
2. **Buying moment:** What has happened that makes this reader seek help now?
3. **Desired result:** What becomes better after reading and applying the book?
4. **Present pain:** What frustration, cost, risk, limitation, or failed alternative do they want to escape?
5. **Distinctive idea or mechanism:** What does this book explain or do differently?
6. **Credibility:** What experience, evidence, cases, or honest limits make the promise believable?
7. **Category and tone:** Practical, premium, provocative, reassuring, technical, literary, playful, or another tone?
8. **Comparables and anti-comparables:** Which books should it sit beside, and which should it not resemble?
9. **Marketplace and constraints:** Print, ebook, audiobook, Leanpub, retailer, course ecosystem, series, language, required terms, forbidden terms, or length limits?

Condense the brief into this sentence:

> For **[specific reader]**, this book helps them move from **[pain or current state]** to **[desired result]** through **[distinctive idea or mechanism]**, supported by **[proof or credible boundary]**.

Also identify:

- the primary promise
- the strongest emotional or identity benefit
- the most credible differentiator
- the maximum claim the evidence can honestly support
- the reader's likely words for the pain and desired result

### Step 2: Decide the title and subtitle jobs

Choose one primary job for the title:

- outcome
- desired state
- problem recognition
- identity
- big idea
- mechanism
- curiosity
- memorable metaphor

Choose the most important missing job for the subtitle:

- audience
- concrete outcome
- mechanism
- problem resolved
- practical scope
- evidence or credibility
- constraint removed
- category signal

Do not force every element into the subtitle.

### Step 3: Inspect the market when stakes are high

When current search tools are available and the user wants a serious final decision:

1. Inspect roughly 10 to 20 current direct comparables.
2. Include functional alternatives such as courses, tools, podcasts, or communities solving the same problem.
3. Note repeated promises, category words, subtitle structures, lengths, and audience signals.
4. Search the problem and desired result using reader language.
5. Search exact and near-exact candidate phrases, including likely variants.
6. Check for books, series, podcasts, products, companies, and trademarks that may create confusion.
7. Identify language that is expected, crowded, tired, strongly owned, or relatively open.
8. Verify current retailer and metadata rules from official sources immediately before publication.

Research is for differentiation and comprehension, not copying. A web or marketplace search is not legal clearance.

Without search tools, label the market assessment **provisional**.

### Step 4: Generate broadly without scoring

For a full strategy, privately generate at least 40 rough titles across at least eight of these strategic lanes. Do not display all rough titles unless requested.

1. **Direct outcome**: state the improvement the reader wants.
2. **Desired state**: name a condition or identity the reader wants to inhabit.
3. **Problem escape**: name the costly situation the reader wants to end.
4. **How-to benefit**: promise useful instruction tied to a result.
5. **Distinctive mechanism**: make the author's method or explanatory model the hook.
6. **Contrarian claim or big idea**: challenge an important category assumption.
7. **Reader identity**: help the intended reader recognize themselves.
8. **Question or curiosity gap**: open a relevant unanswered loop.
9. **Coined category or ownable phrase**: name a phenomenon, practice, or framework.
10. **Metaphor**: express the transformation through a concrete image.
11. **Credible specificity**: use a defensible number, timeframe, scope, or removed constraint.
12. **Hybrid**: combine a memorable idea with an explicit reader benefit.

Vary the strategic idea, not merely the wording. Generate titles before subtitles so a weak title is not disguised by explanatory copy.

### Step 5: Curate the title pool

Reduce the rough pool to 10 to 12 titles that represent genuinely different strategies.

Reject candidates that are:

- generic topic labels
- buzzword piles
- difficult to pronounce, spell, or repeat
- clever only after a long explanation
- misleading or unsupported
- too similar to a known competitor
- dependent on the subtitle to become remotely relevant
- accidentally comic, awkward, dated, or machine-generated

For each survivor, identify its intended reader reaction and largest strategic risk.

### Step 6: Write subtitle directions

For each strong title, create at least three subtitle directions:

1. **Most commercial:** strongest clear outcome
2. **Most distinctive:** strongest mechanism or big idea
3. **Most credible:** clearest practical scope and boundary

Useful subtitle architectures include:

- **Audience + outcome:** How [specific reader] can [desired result]
- **Outcome + mechanism:** [Desired result] through [distinctive method]
- **Problem + practical path:** A practical way to [resolve problem] and [reach result]
- **Big idea + application:** Why [central insight] changes how we [important activity]
- **Scope + boundary:** A guide to [outcome] without [feared cost or compromise]
- **Method + audience + result:** The [method] for [audience] who want [result]

These are thinking modules, not formulas to publish unchanged.

Apply five subtitle tests:

- **Addition:** What does the subtitle add that the title did not already say?
- **Cold reader:** Can the intended reader infer the book's value and relevance?
- **Breath:** Can the full pair be said naturally in one introduction?
- **Thumbnail:** Does the title remain meaningful at small size?
- **Proof burden:** Can every number, absolute, speed claim, and "without" promise be defended?

### Step 7: Score serious candidates

Score the best 8 to 12 title-subtitle pairs from 0 to 5 on each criterion, then convert to weighted points out of 100.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Desired outcome and reader self-interest | 20 |
| Cold-reader clarity | 15 |
| Differentiation and ownability | 15 |
| Specificity and tangibility | 10 |
| Memorability, rhythm, and speakability | 10 |
| Credibility and claim fit | 10 |
| Audience and category fit | 10 |
| Title-subtitle complementarity | 5 |
| Thumbnail and marketplace fitness | 5 |

Use two perspectives where possible:

1. **Strategist:** positioning, differentiation, category fit, and commercial clarity
2. **Skeptical cold reader:** likely interpretation, credibility, appeal, and memory

Average the totals and inspect disagreements. Randomize candidate order or use a fresh-context evaluator when available.

#### Rating anchors

- **5:** exceptional for the category and needs little explanation
- **4:** strong, with a minor weakness or untested assumption
- **3:** workable, but familiar, incomplete, or execution-dependent
- **2:** weak because of confusion, genericness, mismatch, or credibility
- **1:** severe problem that substantially undermines the pair
- **0:** misleading, unusable, irrelevant, or incomprehensible

#### Hard gates

Do not recommend a candidate as publication-ready when any gate fails:

- **Truth:** The book can defend the promise.
- **Comprehension:** A target reader forms a useful and mostly correct expectation.
- **Complementarity:** The subtitle adds information.
- **Conflict awareness:** No known direct collision has been ignored.
- **Speakability:** The pair can be pronounced and repeated naturally.
- **Marketplace fit:** It complies with current rules and accurately represents the book.

A failed gate is not balanced out by a high memorability score.

#### Penalty flags

Apply judgment rather than stacking mechanically:

- unsupported numerical, absolute, or speed claim: reject or minus 10 to 25
- title and subtitle substantially repeat each other: minus 5 to 12
- generic marketing or LLM phrase without fresh specificity: minus 3 to 10
- strong resemblance to a direct competitor: reject or minus 10 to 20
- awkward grammar or pronunciation: minus 5 to 15
- topic label with no promise, tension, identity, or idea: minus 5 to 15
- metadata stuffing or unrelated keywords: reject
- excessive length or poor thumbnail readability: minus 2 to 8

Treat candidates within three points as tied until tested.

### Step 8: Red-team the finalists

For each finalist, answer:

- What will a cold reader think this book delivers?
- Who will they think it is for?
- What intended interpretation is attractive?
- What wrong interpretation is plausible?
- Which word feels generic, inflated, awkward, dated, or machine-generated?
- Is the promise believable from this book and author?
- Can someone say it once and recall or repeat it later?
- Does it remain legible and meaningful at thumbnail size?
- Does the subtitle clarify rather than echo?
- What current marketplace, title, series, product, company, or trademark check remains?

Do not confuse an exact-title search with legal clearance.

### Step 9: Present a useful decision

For a full strategy, present:

1. **Naming brief:** reader, buying moment, desired result, pain, distinctive idea, proof boundary, tone, and constraints
2. **Market pattern:** common category signals, tired language, reader language, open territory, and conflicts, but only when researched
3. **Candidate map:** 10 to 12 survivors labeled by strategic lane, intended reaction, and risk
4. **Shortlist:** 3 to 5 title-subtitle pairs with score, strength, cold-reader interpretation, and main risk
5. **Recommendation:** one current lead and why it leads
6. **Strategically different alternates:** at least two options that win for different reasons
7. **Subtitle refinements:** commercial, distinctive, and credible variants for the lead title
8. **Red-team findings:** likely misunderstanding, credibility issue, or conflict still to check
9. **Validation plan:** a small blind reader or behavioral test

For a fast sprint, provide fewer candidates but preserve strategic diversity. Do not dump dozens of near-duplicates.

For an audit, use this structure for each candidate:

- job it is trying to do
- what works
- what is missing
- likely cold-reader interpretation
- credibility or conflict risk
- best revision that preserves its useful core

## Validation method

A model cannot reliably simulate the whole market. Validate 3 to 5 strategically different finalists with people resembling the actual buyer.

Present candidates:

- in randomized order
- with identical typography and design
- without explaining the intended meaning
- without revealing the author's favorite
- individually before group discussion

Ask:

1. Which book would you investigate first, and why?
2. What do you expect each book to help you do, change, or understand?
3. Who do you think each book is for?
4. Which title feels most credible and least credible?
5. Which word or phrase attracted, repelled, or confused you?
6. What would you search for if you had this problem?
7. After a delay, which titles or phrases can you remember?

Optional 1 to 5 ratings:

- relevance to me
- clarity
- credibility
- distinctiveness
- desire to learn more

When justified, run a behavioral test using identical landing pages, ads, newsletter treatments, or waitlist pages, changing only the title pair.

Use this decision rule:

> A finalist must achieve acceptable comprehension and credibility before preference, recall, or click performance can make it the winner.

Label evidence honestly:

- **Diagnostic:** reveals confusion or useful language
- **Directional:** suggests a likely leader
- **Decision-grade:** sufficiently representative and powered for the commercial stakes

## Anti-patterns

### Topic label instead of promise

> Personal AI Context Management

Accurate but bureaucratic. It names the mechanism before the reader feels the benefit.

### Ultimate-guide reflex

> The Ultimate Guide to Leadership

"Ultimate" creates a large proof burden and no differentiation.

### Buzzword compression

> Agentic AI Transformation: Revolutionizing Enterprise Innovation Through Next-Generation Intelligent Automation

Every word signals a category, but none creates a concrete, distinctive reader promise.

### Subtitle mirror

> Calm Leadership: How to Lead With Calm

The subtitle adds nothing.

### Clever only after explanation

> The Purple Staircase

A metaphor is weak when no target reader can infer relevance until hearing a long story.

### Unsupported precision

> 10X Your Income in 30 Days

Attention does not excuse an unsubstantiated promise.

### Method before desire

> The Seven-Layer Recursive Context Framework

Unless readers already want that framework, lead with the result and place the mechanism in the subtitle.

### Short but empty

> Thrive

Compact does not mean distinctive or relevant.

### False diversity

- Own Your AI
- Take Ownership of AI
- Your Own AI
- AI You Own
- Own the AI

This is one strategic lane, not five.

## Compact example

Brief: A practical book helps knowledge workers organize the prompts, preferences, workflows, skills, and knowledge they create with LLMs into a portable Markdown-based personal context system.

Weak topic label:

> **Personal AI Context Management**  
> A Comprehensive Guide to Prompts, Skills, Markdown, Memory, and LLM Workflows

Why it struggles: accurate but bureaucratic, mechanism-first, and keyword-heavy.

Direct agency direction:

> **Own Your AI**  
> Build a Personal Context System That Knows You, Works Your Way, and Travels With You

Strength: compact agency and a concrete practical promise. Risk: "own" may imply local model ownership or intellectual property.

Human relationship direction:

> **An AI of Your Own**  
> Build a Personal AI That Knows You, Works Your Way, and Runs Without You

Strength: warm, memorable, and personally shaped. Risk: "runs without you" may imply more autonomy than the manuscript supports.

Mechanism direction:

> **The Context You Keep**  
> Turn Your Prompts, Preferences, and Workflows Into a Portable Personal AI System

Strength: names the neglected asset and practical transformation. Risk: less immediate desire than the ownership titles.

These candidates are useful together because they offer different reasons to care: agency, relationship, and mechanism.

## Final quality gate

Before answering, confirm that:

- a cold reader can explain the promised value
- the intended reader recognizes themselves
- the title expresses desire, pain, identity, tension, mechanism, or a meaningful idea rather than merely naming the topic
- title and subtitle do different jobs
- the promise is supportable by the manuscript
- the pair is easy to say, spell, search, and remember
- the shortlist is strategically diverse
- generic marketing and default LLM language have been cut
- current research status is labeled honestly
- marketplace rules and conflicts are checked before publication
- the recommendation explains both upside and risk
- no title is described as a guaranteed winner
