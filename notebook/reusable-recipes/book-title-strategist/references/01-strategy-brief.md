# Strategy Brief

## Core principle

A book title is not a compressed table of contents. It is the shortest useful expression of why the right reader should care.

A practical heuristic is:

> Title strength = wanted value x reader recognition x differentiation x credibility x recall

This is not literal mathematics. It is a reminder that a serious weakness in any one factor can sink an otherwise attractive title.

## The reader-first brief

Capture these fields before generating titles.

### 1. Book job

Identify what the book must do commercially:

- Sell itself at retail
- Generate qualified leads
- Establish authority
- Define or own a category
- Support a course, consulting offer, or movement
- Teach a technical subject
- Share a memoir, argument, or worldview

The same manuscript can require a different title depending on the job.

### 2. Specific reader

Describe one recognizable reader, not a demographic fog.

Useful details include role, life stage, level of sophistication, urgent situation, existing beliefs, and words they use. The title should make the intended reader feel, "This was made for me," without needing to name every demographic detail.

### 3. Present situation

State the problem, frustration, aspiration, transition, or unanswered question already occupying the reader's mind.

### 4. Wanted result

Separate four layers:

- **Functional result:** What changes in practical terms?
- **Emotional result:** How do they want to feel?
- **Identity result:** Who do they want to become?
- **Social result:** How do they want others to see them?

The main title usually carries one strong layer. The subtitle can supply another.

### 5. Avoided cost

What does the reader fear losing or enduring: time, money, status, health, autonomy, relationships, complexity, embarrassment, or opportunity?

### 6. Distinctive mechanism or thesis

Name what makes the book's route to the result different:

- A proprietary framework
- A practical system
- A contrarian thesis
- A new category
- A unique source of evidence
- An unusual story or perspective
- A specific tool, platform, or professional context

Do not force the mechanism into the main title when the reader does not yet care about it. It often belongs in the subtitle.

### 7. Proof

List evidence that calibrates how boldly the title may promise:

- Author experience or credentials
- Documented client outcomes
- Case studies
- Research or data
- A repeatable framework
- A completed transformation
- A body of practical examples

A title may be emotionally bold while remaining factually honest. Specific numerical or time-bound claims require stronger support.

### 8. Voice and status signal

Decide how the book should feel:

- Practical or visionary
- Warm or authoritative
- Mainstream or contrarian
- Premium or accessible
- Technical or plainspoken
- Calm or provocative
- Timeless or trend-aware

### 9. Market landscape

Record:

- 10 to 30 close or adjacent titles when research is available
- Repeated words and structures
- Category conventions readers rely on
- Phrases that feel tired or machine-generated
- Underused territory
- Titles that could create confusion

Do not reject every familiar word. Some familiarity helps category recognition. The goal is recognizable but not interchangeable.

## Positioning sentence

Create one sentence before naming:

> For [specific reader] who [present situation], this book helps them [wanted result] without [feared cost] through [distinctive mechanism or thesis], supported by [proof].

Then identify:

- The one phrase the title should make the reader think
- The one misunderstanding the title must prevent
- The one distinction competitors do not own

## Why "Double Your Dating" is instructive

The title demonstrates several useful mechanics:

- **Double** gives the reader a concrete direction of change.
- **Your** makes the benefit personal.
- **Dating** names the desired domain in ordinary language.
- The repeated initial sound and compact rhythm aid recall.
- The title sells the result rather than the author's internal methodology.

Use these mechanics as inspiration, not as a template to copy. A numerical promise must fit the market, the manuscript, and the available evidence. Some professional categories also require a calmer level of directness.

## Cold-reader test

Before approving a direction, show only the title and subtitle and ask:

1. What do you think this book will help you do or understand?
2. Who do you think it is for?
3. What makes you believe or doubt the promise?
4. What other book does it remind you of?

If the answers diverge sharply from the brief, the title is not clear enough, regardless of how much the author likes it.
