# Validation

LLM judgment narrows the field. It does not prove what the market will prefer.

## 1. Current market scan

When web access is available, search the likely buying environment using the target reader's words.

Review roughly 15 to 30 close and adjacent books when the decision matters. Capture:

- Exact title and subtitle
- Category and intended reader
- Repeated words and title structures
- Strong category cues worth preserving
- Cliches and crowded territory
- Distinctive mechanisms or concepts competitors own
- Exact and near collisions with finalists
- Whether a proposed phrase is already strongly associated with another author, product, or company

Use retailer listings, publisher pages, library catalogs, author pages, and official databases. Search variations in spelling, word order, singular/plural, and sound.

Do not conclude that a title is legally clear because a quick search found nothing.

## 2. Rights and trademark check

For a serious finalist:

- Search exact and near-identical book titles.
- Search major web and social platforms.
- Search official trademark databases in the markets where the book will be sold.
- Check distinctive coined terms, framework names, and branded phrases more carefully than ordinary descriptive wording.
- Record unresolved risks.

This is preliminary screening, not legal advice or a professional clearance opinion.

## 3. Metadata and cover check

Before locking the title:

- Confirm that title and subtitle match the cover and publishing metadata.
- Verify current platform character limits and prohibited metadata.
- Avoid promotional claims such as sales rank, free offers, or unauthorized references.
- Check the cover at small thumbnail size.
- Check spine readability.
- Read the pair aloud in an introduction: "The author wrote..."
- Ask whether a listener can spell and search it correctly.

## 4. Blind reader test

Shortlist three to five strategically different title-subtitle pairs. Randomize their order and show them without explaining the author's favorite.

Recruit people who resemble the intended reader, not only friends or fellow authors.

Ask:

1. Which book would you investigate first?
2. What do you expect each book to help you do, understand, or experience?
3. Who do you think each book is for?
4. Which promise feels most credible?
5. Which title feels most distinctive rather than generic?
6. Which title can you remember later without looking?
7. What, if anything, feels confusing, exaggerated, or unattractive?

Preference alone is not enough. A popular title that creates the wrong expectation is a bad result.

## 5. Decision measures

Use four main measures:

- **Preference:** Would the reader click, pick up, or investigate it?
- **Correct comprehension:** Do they understand the intended promise?
- **Credibility:** Do they believe the book can deliver?
- **Recall:** Can they remember and repeat it later?

A simple directional weighting is:

- Preference: 30%
- Correct comprehension: 30%
- Credibility: 20%
- Recall: 20%

Change the weighting when the book's job demands it. A technical reference book may weight comprehension more heavily. A thought-leadership book may weight distinctiveness and recall more heavily.

## 6. Behavioral testing

When the author has an audience or traffic, test finalists through real behavior rather than stated preference alone:

- Landing-page variants
- Email subject or click tests leading to concept pages
- Small, policy-compliant ad tests
- Webinar or lead-magnet registration pages
- Cover thumbnail tests

Keep every element except the title treatment as constant as practical. Do not overinterpret tiny samples.

## 7. Final decision rule

Choose the title that best combines:

1. Strong reader interest
2. Correct understanding
3. Credible promise
4. Distinctive memory
5. Faithfulness to the book
6. Acceptable market and rights risk

Document why the winner was chosen and what evidence remains uncertain.
