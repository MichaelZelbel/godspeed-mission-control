# Finding three to five picture ideas for a post

The proposing step is the one that decides whether the picture is any good. Rendering is cheap and
reliable; choosing the object is the whole craft. This file is the method.

Read [observations/gary-vaynerchuk-visual-language.md](../../../observations/gary-vaynerchuk-visual-language.md)
first if you have not. It is the evidence behind everything below.

## The one question

**What object already argues this sentence, before anybody reads a word?**

Not "what could illustrate this". Illustration is the failure. A post about being interrupted does
not get a picture of somebody looking annoyed; it gets a doorstop wedged under a door that has been
kicked open anyway. The object has to make the argument on its own, so that the words are a caption
on something already true rather than an explanation of something arbitrary.

## The test that kills bad ideas early

**Could the words be deleted in Photoshop, leaving a clean background?**

If yes, the idea has failed and no amount of rendering saves it. In a good one the words are
stitched, burned, scratched, iced, hammered or worn into the object, so removing them would leave
holes. Apply this test to the IDEA, before writing a recipe, because it is free at that stage.

## The cold read: what a stranger thinks happened

Every other test here is scored by someone who already knows the post, and that person sees
the metaphor because they are looking for it. A stranger scrolling past has not read the post.
They see a scene and, without meaning to, tell themselves the story of how it came to be.

So for each idea, before anything else, write one sentence: **"A stranger who has not read the
post thinks this happened: ..."** Write the literal story of the scene, not the metaphor. If the
story involves illness, drugs, danger, dirt, damage, neglect or somebody being stupid, the idea is
dead, however well it matches the post.

Examples of a plausible metaphor failing the literal-scene test:

- a wooden elephant holding tablets by a bed: a sick person, maybe an addict, a cry for help
- a sticking plaster on a vinyl record: a stupid bodge that will break the needle
- latte art reading "the usual" on a stained cup and saucer: a dirty cup handed to you
- a dog holding a blister strip: the owner failed, and the dog is about to eat the pills and die

What follows from that:

- **Medicine is a trap object.** Tablets in a picture read as sickness or drugs, never as care.
  A dog or a child near tablets reads as danger.
- **A fix on the wrong object reads as a bodge.** A plaster belongs on skin; anywhere else it
  says someone made it worse.
- **Worn is not dirty.** `worn_and_real` means scuffs and wear on tools, wood, cloth and paper.
  Anything that touches food or a mouth must be clean, or it reads as disgusting.

## Where to look for the object

In this order, because this is the order that produces distinct ideas rather than five versions of
one idea:

1. **The object that does the thing.** The sentence describes an action; find the tool that performs
   it. Surrender is a white flag. Persistence is a punching bag. Waiting is a ticket stub.
2. **The object that suffered the thing.** Not the actor, the victim. A worn doormat, a chewed pen,
   a transcript with Ds on it.
3. **The object that contradicts the thing.** Comfort placed on discomfort: a soft pillow on a bed
   of nails. The contradiction does the arguing.
4. **The scale joke.** Borrow a diagram convention, a leader line pointing at a speck, and let the
   proportion be the point.
5. **The artefact.** Sometimes the real document IS the argument and needs no sentence at all.

Five sources, three to five proposals. If two proposals come from the same source they are the same
idea twice, and one should be replaced.

## What a proposal contains, and what it does not

A proposal is four short fields. It is deliberately **not** a recipe, because a recipe is long and
four out of five proposals are never rendered.

| field | length | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| `label` | two or three words | A name to pick by. "The white flag". |
| `pitch` | one sentence | What you see, and why that IS the sentence. Both halves, or it is not a pitch. |
| `object` | a phrase | The physical thing, concretely enough to photograph. |
| `risk` | one sentence | What will most likely go wrong in the render, stated in advance. |

The `risk` field earns its place. Writing it forces the choice to be examined before money is
spent, and it tells the person choosing what they are signing up for. Real risks that recur:
lettering that comes back printed instead of made-of-the-material; a robot that drifts into science
fiction; an object so unusual the model renders a plausible neighbour instead; text the model
misspells because there is too much of it.

## The sentence on the object

- **Five to ten words.** One sentence. No subhead, no second line, no source, no hashtags.
- **Case it the way its speaker would type it.** A line quoted from an assistant is sentence case
  with a full stop, because no assistant has ever answered in capitals, and the wrong casing
  destroys the recognition that the whole picture depends on. A line the author says out loud may
  be capitals.
- **Never ask for a font.** The moment the words are typed rather than made, the picture is a
  template with the words swapped, and that is the look this whole method exists to avoid.

## Presenting them

Numbered list, one line each: the label, then the pitch, then the risk in brackets. the user picks
by number and can pick more than one. Do not render before he has picked, and do not render all
five to "let him see them", which spends five times the money to answer a question a sentence
answers.

## After the pick

Write the full recipe for each picked idea against
[recipe-v1.md](recipe-v1.md), then render. The proposal's `object` becomes `subject`, the `pitch`
informs `composition`, and the `risk` usually turns into an extra line in `strict_negatives`.
