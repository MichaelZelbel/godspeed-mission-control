# Chapter 9: Keep Your AI's Information Up to Date

The project is finished. You know it, the client knows it, and everyone has moved on except the file that still calls it your top priority. Old notes can be remarkably loyal to a life you no longer lead. Unless the note changes, the assistant can keep planning a week you no longer need.

Begin when an answer uses old information or you know something has changed. That gives the review a purpose, without adding a cleaning routine for every file. Ask the assistant to compare the profile files before it edits anything:

```
Read the current files in profile/, excluding interview prompts, checklists and templates.

Describe my life and work from those files only: who I am, who matters, active work, priorities and how I want help.

Then list possible conflicts, dates that may be stale, and claims too vague to use. Quote the conflicting lines and name their files. Separate what the files establish from what needs my confirmation.

Do not search elsewhere or change anything yet. Unchanged information is not automatically outdated.
```

## Read the conflicts, not just the portrait

The first part shows you the life your files describe. Read it for recognition, then pay closer attention to the disagreements below it. Comparing notes written weeks apart can reveal a change that never reached the other files.

In Sam's July files, the card-set deadline appeared in two forms:

> **"End of the month" vs 2026-07-28.** `about-me.md` says the card set is due "end of the month". `projects.md` pins it to Tuesday 2026-07-28. Those are 3 days apart. Which one is right?

This is worth a question. “End of the month” may have been a loose way of describing the agreed date; either note could also be wrong. A short answer from Sam would settle it. Quietly choosing a date would only hide the doubt.

The same review found a person missing from a related file:

> **Third regular client missing.** `about-me.md` lists three regulars: board-game studio, children's book publisher, and a coffee brand. `people.md` has no coffee brand entry. Either the coffee brand is inactive or the file is incomplete.

A missing client entry deserves a question, not a completed biography. Sam may have left someone out, or the relationship may have changed. The review earns its keep by bringing him the gap while those explanations are still possibilities.

## Make the smallest useful correction

Most findings need one of four moves:

1. **Close completed work.** Remove it from the active list and keep a dated record if its history matters.
2. **Replace a changed value.** Update the actual deadline or budget where it lives. Do not leave two values presented as current.
3. **Combine vague or repeated notes.** Keep the useful detail and its source. Remove repetition that adds no information.
4. **Confirm slow changes.** Check your background and priorities when they no longer fit. They may remain accurate for months.

Tell the assistant which corrections to make, then read the changed lines. If you have changed direction on something important, keep the date and reason in `decisions.md`. Your future self may be grateful for more than “seemed like a good idea.”

Use the kit's `profile/spring-clean-checklist.md` if you want a reminder of what to review. Once the briefing matches your life, stop. An accurate file is allowed to sit untouched for months; it doesn't need a recent edit to prove that you care.

## Check the cause of a poor answer

An answer can be wrong even when the file is right. The assistant may have missed it, misread your request or made a poor judgment. Before editing the note, find out which happened. Changing correct information to satisfy a bad answer would give you two problems.

Ask the assistant to show the file and line it used, then compare that source with its claim. If it correctly copied a budget that has since changed, the budget needs updating. If it misread the current figure, ask it to trace where its answer departed from the source and correct that mistake.

## Check the next use

After changing a priority, open a fresh session and ask for advice it should affect. Look for the new information in the answer and ask to see its source. That is a more useful result than another cheerful confirmation that the file has been saved.

Deleting a line removes it from the current briefing. Earlier Git versions, conversation records and provider copies may still hold it. If you need information removed everywhere, ask the assistant to identify those locations and their deletion controls before changing anything. A corrected current note and complete deletion are different jobs.

Keep corrections small enough to make when you notice the change, and check that the next answer uses them. If making those corrections has turned into arguing with a text box, Chapter 10 is a short detour you may recognise. Otherwise, continue the build in Chapter 11.
