# Chapter 7: Teach AI Your Writing Style

The draft has every fact right. It also greets your old friend as though applying for a job with her. You remove the formal opening, cut two apologies and rewrite half the message. Tomorrow, the assistant proudly gives you the same voice again.

“Friendly but professional” leaves an awful lot to guess. Give the assistant a few examples of your own writing and it can see what those words mean when you write to a client, an old friend or someone you need to disappoint. That is a better starting point than another adjective.

## Supply three to five examples

Choose three to five short pieces of your own writing. Ordinary emails make good samples, as do explanations, refusals and messages with bad news. Pick pieces where you recognise your own voice.

Remove private details before giving the samples to Mission Control. It can learn your sentence length and tone without somebody's real name or access details coming along for the lesson. Then ask it to save each sample as a separate text file in `inbox/writing-samples/`, creating the folder if needed.

Then paste:

```
Read the files in inbox/writing-samples/ and the current profile/voice.md. Propose a short set of writing rules based on the examples.

Cover sentence length, openings and closings, directness, bad news, spelling, familiar phrases and phrases to avoid. Quote a sample line where it supports a rule. Label uncertain patterns; a few examples do not establish what I always or never write.

Keep explicit preferences I already confirmed separate from patterns you infer. Do not flatter me. Finish with a short list of phrases or habits to avoid.

Show the proposed profile/voice.md, then save the version I confirm. Keep the writing samples.
```

## Keep what you recognise

In Sam's demonstration, the assistant proposed concrete rules such as:

> Numbers are always digits, including small ones.

> British spelling: `colour`, `favourite`, `apologise`, `organise`.

> Never apologise more than once, and never in the opening line.

A rule like that gives you something to agree with or correct. The assistant has only seen a few examples, so check how far each rule should apply. If you write numbers differently in reports and personal messages, say so before a habit from one kind of writing becomes a rule for all of it.

My run produced more than a hundred lines. Apparently, being me was going to require a manual. I cut the repeated points and the rules I did not recognise, leaving a shorter page to compare with the way I actually write.

Keep a short list of phrases you find yourself deleting. When one returns in the next draft, you can point to the rule and ask for a correction. Then read the revised message as a message: removing the forbidden words is useful, but the remaining sentences still have to sound like you.

## Try one real draft

Try the guide on a real message to someone in your files. Ask for a draft without explaining your style again, and leave it unsent while you compare it with your samples. Notice what you still want to change. I would trust that reaction over the assistant's cheerful verdict on how well it has captured your voice.

The book's practice request was:

```
Draft the message to Nadia about the four-day slip on cards 8 to 12.
```

The assistant needed the people, project and writing files to prepare this reply.

Two excerpts from the draft:

> Quick heads up while there's still room to move it.

> I'd rather tell you now than hand you a surprise on the 28th. Say which you prefer and I'll lock it in today.

That last line promises action today, so it needs Sam's agreement before sending. A sentence can sound wonderfully like you and still promise something you never agreed to.

The draft also drew on a note in Sam's files: giving Nadia three options cut the number of revision rounds on an earlier job. That was background supplied for the practice run. Your assistant needs an equivalent note from your actual work before it can use such a lesson.

The most useful part of this draft was the gap it kept:

> I left the reason as a placeholder because projects.md doesn't record why cards 8 to 12 slipped and I don't want to invent one.

The guide shaped the wording, but Sam's files still had no explanation for the delay. The assistant left that part for him to supply. It is a relief to receive a draft that has not improved your life story while improving your sentences.

## Save the correction, not just the better sentence

Tell the assistant what to correct and ask it to save the lesson in the relevant file. A phrase you keep cutting goes into the writing guide; a wrong date belongs in the project file. If the assistant invented an explanation, deal with the missing facts before polishing the sentence. A better voice cannot make the excuse true.

The guide can grow with the writing you do, using new samples when a different kind of message comes along. You'll still edit drafts, but a correction saved here gives the assistant something to use the next time it writes for you.
