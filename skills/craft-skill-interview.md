# Chapter 14: Create Skills for Your Own Work

The email recipes cover familiar chores. Your own work will have more particular ones: preparing a lesson, checking a proposal, or turning rough notes into a report somebody needs. Look for a job you can explain clearly because you've explained it far too often.

I recommend starting with work you know well. Your experience tells you which details matter and where an answer falls short, including the parts that look fine to someone outside your field. Let the assistant put the method into words, then use that experience to judge what it missed.

## Find the candidates

Start with this interview:

```
I want to build skills for my specific job: instructions saved in skills/<name>/SKILL.md and reused. Read my existing profile and work records first. Interview me only about what those records cannot answer, to find up to three useful candidates.

Ask one question at a time, five questions maximum, about:

- the tasks I repeat every week
- the ones I always explain the same way
- the ones I dread

Then propose up to three skills, each with a name and what it takes in and gives back. Do not invent a third candidate to fill the list.
```

*[Copy prompt](https://srv1328602.hstgr.cloud/hub/8d0da988c1c44fbaa71bdfef1d4144dc/prompts.html#chapter-15-box-1)*

Two questions from the interview were especially useful:

> What's the task you actively put off, the one that sits on your list for days because it feels tedious, awkward, or draining? Name a specific instance from the last month.

> What's the thing you catch yourself explaining or correcting to me (or to a tool) more than once, the same nudge, same fix, same "no, do it this way"? Give the most recent example.

A task you keep avoiding deserves a closer look, although boredom and difficulty call for different help. Repeated paperwork can be a good candidate for a draft; a difficult conversation may need preparation that leaves the conversation itself to you. Identify the part the assistant could usefully do. The task doesn't have to disappear completely to become less of a burden.

## Use three filters

**The input already exists.** Begin with something the work already produces, such as an email, project file or set of notes. Rebuilding the background for the assistant every time is work this skill won't save you.

**The output is something you need.** A draft your colleague expects has a reason to exist. Another report that nobody will open gives you more to produce and store, however neatly it is arranged.

**You can judge the result and keep the authority.** The assistant may compare, criticise or recommend. It does not acquire permission to make a commitment.

If one proposal fits, build that one. The interview offers a few candidates so you have a choice. Accepting help shouldn't require accepting three new tasks as well.

## The illustrator's example

Sam, our fictional illustrator, kept making two small corrections: don't promise a date I haven't approved, and stop apologising twice. His weekly update needed news about the cards, not another apology and a date he hadn't chosen. Saving those corrections would spare him the same repair in every draft.

Here is part of what the assistant proposed:

> **1. `draft-weekly-client-update.md`** In: client name, any status not in `profile/projects.md` yet. Out: a draft message with three sections, what's done, what's next, what I need from you, grounded only in `projects.md` and what you just told me, with no promised delivery dates and no double apology.

It also suggested checking drafts for unsupported delivery promises and repeated apologies, then preparing replies to changes requested after approval. Sam's example allowed 20 minutes for a small free change. Use the allowance agreed for your own work; an illustrator in a book shouldn't be setting your prices.

“After approval” sounds clear until you ask what was approved and by whom. Give the skill the agreed scope, who may approve a change, and whether its job ends with a draft. People can agree with the same phrase while meaning quite different things.

## Build the chosen skill

Once you've chosen, ask the assistant to write the full `skills/<name>/SKILL.md` and show you the result. The name and description help it select the skill; the sources and answer rules guide the work. Read those parts against the job you described. Leave sending, publishing and other external actions out unless you deliberately authorise them, so the first version gives you work to review before anything leaves your hands.

I chose Sam's weekly update. The resulting file included these instructions:

```
4. Never put a delivery date in a client message unless I said the date myself in the current update. Do not turn a date in `profile/projects.md` into a client-facing delivery date.

5. Never apologise twice in one message. Use no more than one apology, and do not open with it.
```

The second sentence of rule 4 catches a distinction Sam cares about. The date in his private project file can be a target he is working towards, without being a promise he is ready to send Nadia. Recording that difference saves him from having to explain it with every draft.

These are two rules from the skill created in that test. A complete skill also needs to describe the input, the sources to read and what to do when information is missing.

## Give the first run something real to do

Give the skill a normal example with enough detail to do the job, then try again with a needed fact removed. Watch what it does with the gap. A question or a clearly marked blank lets you supply the answer; a plausible invention leaves you hunting for a mistake in a sentence that looks finished.

A skill can help you review work as well as draft it. A teacher could test one that compares a lesson with what students have already learned; a writer could ask where a chapter loses sight of its promise to the reader. Try such suggestions against your own knowledge. It gives you a way to judge the criticism as well as the draft.

Ask the assistant to put corrections to repeated habits in the skill and this week's changed facts in the project record. That lets the method stay useful as the work moves on. Otherwise, last week's unfinished business can keep reappearing in a recipe you meant to use for months.

Save the full file where you can open it, then give it a job in a fresh conversation. Read the result before adding the explanation you gave during the interview. The gap between those two moments shows you what still needs to reach the instructions.
